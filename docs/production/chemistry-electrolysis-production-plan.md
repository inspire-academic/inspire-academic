# Chemistry Production Plan — Electrolysis

**Run type:** first genuinely new Chemistry production lesson through
Factory v0. This is not a pilot or rehydration run.

## A. Curriculum / specification mapping

- Repository mapping: Chemistry → `chemical-changes`; AQA
  `aqa-ch-fh-chemical-changes` (Electrolysis), Edexcel
  `edx-ch-fh-ionic-equations` (Electrolysis principles; industrial
  electrolysis).
- Shared core: electrolytes; molten/dissolved ionic compounds; direct
  current; cathode/anode polarity; cation/anion migration; discharge;
  molten binary products; aqueous product selection with inert electrodes;
  oxidation and reduction at electrodes.
- Higher-assessed: constructing and balancing half-equations. AQA explicitly
  marks this Higher-only; it remains available as Higher depth for Edexcel.
- Required-practical relationship: AQA requires aqueous electrolysis with
  inert electrodes. Edexcel specifies a copper sulfate electrolysis core
  practical. This lesson teaches the conceptual/product-prediction knowledge
  that underpins both but does not attempt to replace either practical lesson.
- Application bridge: aluminium is included only to explain why very reactive
  metals require electrolysis. Detailed cryolite/carbon-anode process teaching
  is deferred because the boards' required depth diverges.
- Excluded: electroplating (Edexcel separate Chemistry), copper purification
  (Edexcel-specific depth), quantitative Faraday calculations, and detailed
  industrial cell engineering.

## B. Prerequisites

- Ionic compounds contain positive and negative ions.
- Ionic charge notation and simple formulae.
- Solid ionic lattices have fixed ions; molten/dissolved ions can move.
- Basic reactivity series position relative to hydrogen.
- Elements in Group 7 form diatomic halogen molecules.

## C. Learning objectives

1. Define electrolysis and electrolyte accurately.
2. Explain ion movement to the cathode and anode without claiming electrons
   move through the electrolyte.
3. Predict products from molten binary ionic compounds.
4. Predict products from aqueous solutions with inert electrodes using a
   transparent decision rule.
5. Explain reduction at the cathode and oxidation at the anode.
6. Higher: construct and balance electrode half-equations in unfamiliar cases.

## D. Foundation mastery expectation

Given a named molten compound or a supported aqueous electrolyte, identify the
ions, send cations to the negative cathode and anions to the positive anode,
then use the provided aqueous rules to predict both products. State that
reduction is gain and oxidation is loss of electrons.

## E. Higher mastery expectation

Reason through competing ions in an unfamiliar aqueous electrolyte, justify
which species is discharged, and construct atom- and charge-balanced
half-equations without copying a template.

## F. Central misconceptions

1. Electrons travel through the electrolyte (they do not; mobile ions carry
   charge there).
2. Positive ions go to the positive electrode (opposite charges attract).
3. Cathode/anode polarity is reversed in an electrolytic cell.
4. Atoms, rather than ions, migrate through the electrolyte.
5. Molten and aqueous electrolysis give identical products.
6. The written salt formula alone determines aqueous products; water's H⁺ and
   OH⁻ must be considered.
7. Oxidation/reduction is assigned from electrode sign rather than electron
   transfer.
8. Half-equations balance atoms but not charge.

## G. Worked-example sequence

1. Foundation-only concrete orientation: molten sodium chloride, with one ion
   choice at each electrode.
2. Molten lead bromide: predict products, including diatomic bromine.
3. Aqueous copper(II) chloride with inert electrodes: list four ions, apply
   both discharge rules, predict copper and chlorine.
4. Aqueous sodium sulfate with inert electrodes: explain why water-derived
   ions produce hydrogen and oxygen.
5. Higher: independently construct and charge-check both half-equations for
   molten magnesium chloride.

Each follows question → known information → reasoning → working → answer →
common wrong approach → why wrong.

## H. Assessment progression

- Retrieval: ionic charge, mobility, conductivity, reactivity and diatomic
  halogens.
- Guided: electrode identity → ion direction → molten products → supported
  aqueous competition.
- Independent: mixed molten/aqueous cases with no hints and unfamiliar ions.
- Exam practice: AO1 definition, AO2 product prediction, AO2 explanation,
  AO3 critique of flawed reasoning, and Higher half-equation transfer.
- Exit retrieval and separate Foundation mastery/confidence review.

## I. AO opportunities

- AO1: definitions, electrode polarity, OIL RIG, discharge rules.
- AO2: apply rules to named molten and aqueous electrolytes.
- AO3: analyse a student's incorrect particle/electron model and evaluate a
  product prediction from observations.

## J. Representation requirements and routing

1. **Mode A — deterministic SVG:** complete electrolytic cell showing power
   supply, electrode polarity, cation/anion migration, and electron flow only
   in the external circuit.
2. **Mode A — deterministic SVG:** molten-versus-aqueous decision comparison,
   making water-derived competing ions visible.
3. **Mode A — deterministic SVG:** electrode electron-transfer strip linking
   cathode/reduction and anode/oxidation to half-equation placement.

No graph, raster Premium Final Figure, or hybrid overlay is justified.

## K. Reusable assets

- Reuse the frozen Chemistry lesson's theme tokens, responsive figure framing,
  semantic `<figure>`/`<figcaption>` pattern, and accessibility anatomy.
- Do not reuse the Mass–Mole Strip geometry: it cannot express spatial ion
  migration or circuit/electrode relationships accurately.
- Create a narrow Electrolysis Cell representation family in authored inline
  SVG. Extract no generic Chemistry framework.

## L. Board-specific divergence

- AQA: half-equations explicitly Higher-only; detailed aluminium/cryolite/
  carbon-anode content; aqueous inert-electrode required practical.
- Edexcel: half-equations within Electrolytic Processes; named copper sulfate
  core practical with inert and copper electrodes; copper purification;
  electroplating in separate Chemistry.
- This shared lesson includes only common conceptual/product-prediction content
  and labels the limited application/practical bridges honestly.
