Resultant Forces & Free-Body Diagrams

Resultant Forces & Free-Body Diagrams

GCSE Physics · Forces and Motion · Production Pilot #3 AQA · Edexcel Estimated time: 35–45 min

By the end of this lesson you will be able to read and draw free-body diagrams and calculate the resultant force acting on an object — and answer the question this lesson is built around: when several forces act on one object, what single force has the same effect as all of them combined?

  • Combine forces acting in the same direction, and forces acting in opposite directions, to find a resultant force.
  • Read a free-body diagram: an isolated object with arrows showing every force acting on it.
  • Explain the difference between balanced forces (resultant = 0 N) and unbalanced forces (non-zero resultant).
  • Higher Evaluate a free-body diagram for errors, and explain why balanced forces on one object are never the same thing as a Newton's third-law pair.
Foundation pathway — what you need to master

This lesson has one focus for you: being able to work out the resultant force on an object and read a simple free-body diagram, every time, without mixing up balanced and unbalanced. By the end, you should be confident with:

  • Adding two forces that act in the same direction.
  • Subtracting two forces that act in opposite directions, and stating the direction of the answer.
  • Saying whether a pair of forces is balanced (resultant = 0 N) or unbalanced.
  • Reading a simple free-body diagram and naming each force shown.
  • Explaining that balanced forces don't have to mean an object is standing still.

3Core Lesson

First, a concrete example — before any definitions

Imagine two people pushing the same shopping trolley. One pushes with 40 N, the other with 30 N, both in the same direction. The trolley behaves exactly as if a single, bigger push of 70 N were acting on it — that single equivalent push is called the resultant force.

Now imagine instead they push from opposite ends, one with 40 N one way and the other with 30 N the other way. The trolley behaves as if a single 10 N push were acting, in the direction of the stronger person. Same two people, same two numbers — a completely different resultant, because direction matters.

Before this lesson: force, briefly recapped

A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons (N). Like displacement (Lesson 1), a force is a vector — it needs both a size and a direction to be fully described. "500 N" is an incomplete answer; "500 N to the right" is complete.

Stage 1 — Same-direction forces add

When two or more forces act on the same object in the same direction, the resultant force is simply their sum. Two tugboats both pulling a barge forward, one at 4000 N and one at 2500 N, produce a resultant of 6500 N forward — the barge cannot tell the difference between two pulls and one bigger one.

Stage 2 — Opposing forces subtract; balanced and unbalanced

When two forces act in opposite directions, the resultant is the difference between them, in the direction of the larger force. If the two forces are exactly equal, the resultant is 0 N — the forces are balanced. If they're unequal, a resultant remains in the direction of the bigger force — the forces are unbalanced.

Balanced does not mean stationary

Balanced forces (resultant = 0 N) mean the object's motion isn't changing — it could be standing still, or it could be moving at a steady, constant speed in a straight line. Both are consistent with a zero resultant. Only an unbalanced force changes how something is moving.

Stage 3 — Free-body diagrams

A free-body diagram isolates a single object and shows every force acting on it as an arrow — direction shown by the arrow's orientation, and (where the diagram is drawn to scale) magnitude shown by the arrow's length. Common force types you'll meet: weight (the pull of gravity, always downward), normal contact force (a surface pushing back on an object resting on it), friction and drag (resisting motion), and driving force or thrust (pushing an object along).

Key words, in plain terms

Weight = the pull of gravity on an object, always straight down. Normal contact force = a surface pushing back to stop things sinking into it, always at right angles to the surface. Resultant force = the one force that has the same overall effect as every real force added together — it is not an extra force alongside the real ones.

Stage 4 — Balanced forces are not a Newton's third-law pair

This is the single most important distinction in this lesson. Balanced forces act on one object and can cancel to a zero resultant — for example, a book's weight and the normal contact force from the table it rests on. A Newton's third-law pair is a completely different idea: it's the equal-and-opposite pair of forces that arise whenever two different objects interact (the book pushes down on the table exactly as hard as the table pushes up on the book) — always on two separate objects, never one. A book's weight and its normal contact force are balanced forces on the same object, not a third-law pair, because weight and normal contact force are two genuinely different types of force, not the two halves of one interaction.

Stage 5 — Deeper reasoning Higher

A real object usually has both horizontal forces (driving force vs. friction/drag) and vertical forces (weight vs. normal contact force) acting at once. Treat the two directions separately — find the horizontal resultant and the vertical resultant independently; GCSE Physics at this stage does not combine them into a single diagonal resultant. Being able to evaluate a free-body diagram — spot a force that's missing, mislabelled, or drawn the wrong way — is a genuinely different skill from calculating a resultant from forces you're simply handed, and is where real understanding (rather than pattern-matching a formula) shows up.

5Worked Examples

Example 0 — the simplest case, one step at a time Foundation

Question
A sled is pulled by two ropes in the same direction: one team pulls with 90 N, the other with 60 N. Calculate the resultant force.
Step 1
Check the directions: both ropes pull the same way.
Step 2
Same direction means add the two forces together.
Step 3
90 + 60 = 150.
Step 4
State the direction too — a force answer is never complete without one.
Answer
150 N, in the direction both teams pulled.

Common wrong approach: forgetting to state the direction, and writing just "150 N" — an incomplete answer for a vector quantity, the same rule Lesson 1 established for displacement.

Example 1 — Same-direction forces

Question
Two tugboats pull a barge in the same direction: 4000 N and 2500 N. Calculate the resultant force.
What matters
Both forces act in the same direction.
Reasoning
Same-direction forces add.
Calculation
4000 + 2500 = 6500
Answer
6500 N, in the direction both tugs pulled.

Common wrong approach: treating the two tugs as if they were somehow in competition and subtracting one from the other — subtraction is for opposing forces only; forces pulling the same way always add.

Example 2 — Opposing forces

Question
In a tug-of-war, Team A pulls with 600 N and Team B pulls with 550 N in the opposite direction. Calculate the resultant force.
What matters
The two forces act in opposite directions.
Reasoning
Opposing forces subtract; the answer takes the direction of the larger force.
Calculation
600 − 550 = 50
Answer
50 N, in Team A's direction.

Common wrong approach: adding the two forces (1150 N) instead of subtracting — that would be the correct method only if the two teams pulled the same way, which they don't.

Example 3 — Balanced forces at constant velocity

Question
A car travels along a motorway at a steady, unchanging speed. Its driving force is 900 N forward; the combined resistive forces (friction and air resistance) are 900 N backward. Calculate the resultant force, and state what this tells you about the car's motion.
What matters
The two forces are equal and opposite.
Reasoning
Equal, opposing forces are balanced: resultant = 0 N.
Calculation
900 − 900 = 0
Answer
0 N. The car's motion isn't changing — it continues at the same steady speed in the same direction. Balanced forces do not mean the car has stopped.

Common wrong approach: concluding a resultant of 0 N means the car must be stationary — a balanced resultant only means unchanging motion, which includes travelling at a genuine, steady, non-zero speed.

Example 4 — Free-body diagram identification

Question
A book rests on a table. Identify the two forces acting on it, state whether they are balanced, and explain why they are not a Newton's third-law pair.
What matters
Two forces act on the book itself: its weight, and the normal contact force from the table.
Reasoning
Weight (150 N down) and normal contact force (150 N up) are equal and opposite on the same object, so they are balanced (resultant = 0 N). A Newton's third-law pair would need two different objects, each exerting the same type of force on the other — weight and normal contact force are two different force types on one object, not that.
Answer
Weight = 150 N down; normal contact force = 150 N up; balanced (resultant 0 N); not a third-law pair.

Common wrong approach: calling weight and normal contact force a "Newton's third-law pair" because they're equal and opposite — equal-and-opposite alone is not the test; a genuine third-law pair must act on two separate objects.

Example 5 — Multi-force object

Question
A delivery van has a driving force of 2000 N forward and resistive forces of 600 N backward. Separately, its weight is 12000 N down and the normal contact force from the road is 12000 N up. Calculate the horizontal resultant force and the vertical resultant force.
What matters
Two independent force systems: horizontal (driving vs. resistive) and vertical (weight vs. normal contact force).
Reasoning
Treat each direction separately — never combine them into one diagonal resultant at this stage.
Calculation
Horizontal: 2000 − 600 = 1400. Vertical: 12000 − 12000 = 0.
Answer
Horizontal resultant = 1400 N forward. Vertical resultant = 0 N (balanced).

Common wrong approach: mixing the horizontal and vertical numbers together into a single calculation — the two directions are independent and must be resolved separately.

Example 6 — Spot the error Higher

Question
A student draws a free-body diagram for a crate resting undisturbed on a table, showing only one force: a 200 N weight acting downward. Identify the error and explain the correct diagram.
The student's error
The normal contact force from the table has been left out entirely. With only a weight shown, the diagram implies an unbalanced downward resultant force — but the crate is resting undisturbed, so it cannot have an unbalanced force acting on it.
Correct diagram
A second arrow is needed: a 200 N normal contact force, upward, from the table.
Reasoning
200 (weight) − 200 (normal contact force) = 0 — balanced, consistent with the crate staying still.
Answer
The diagram is missing the normal contact force (200 N up); with it included, the forces are balanced.

6Misconception Clinic

Eight specific wrong beliefs this topic invites — each one named, shown why it's wrong, and tied back to a diagram or worked example above.

"If forces are balanced, the object must be stationary."
Balanced forces mean the object's motion is not changing — it could be standing still, or moving at a genuine, steady, constant speed. Only an unbalanced force changes how something is moving. See Worked Example 3, the car at constant velocity.
"A moving object must have a force acting in its direction of motion."
An object moving at a steady speed in a straight line can have balanced forces acting on it — resultant force 0 N — and keep moving anyway. A force is needed to change motion, not to sustain it.
"The resultant force is a separate, additional force acting alongside the real ones."
The resultant is not an extra force — it is the single force that has the same overall effect as every real force added together. A crate with a 500 N force right and a 200 N force left has exactly two real forces acting on it, not three. See Diagram 3, where the resultant is drawn in its own separate row specifically so it's never mistaken for a third arrow acting on the object.
"Equal and opposite arrows on one object always form a Newton's third-law pair."
A Newton's third-law pair always acts on two different objects — never one. Equal-and-opposite forces on the same object (like a book's weight and the table's normal contact force) are balanced forces, a completely different idea. See Core Lesson Stage 4 and Worked Example 4.
"Weight and normal contact force are always a Newton's third-law pair."
They are not — they act on the same object (whatever is resting on the surface) and are two genuinely different types of force. A real third-law pair involves the same type of force acting in both directions between two objects (e.g. the book pushes down on the table exactly as hard as the table pushes up on the book). See Diagram 4.
"Larger or heavier-looking objects always experience a larger resultant force."
The resultant force depends only on the actual labelled forces shown in the diagram, not on how big the object looks. Always add/subtract the real numbers given — never estimate a resultant from an object's apparent size.
"Arrow length always means magnitude, even in a schematic diagram."
Only in a diagram explicitly drawn to scale does arrow length represent magnitude. A schematic diagram (like Diagram 1) shows direction only — its arrow lengths carry no magnitude information at all, and must never be compared to each other.
"Friction always exactly equals the driving force."
Friction only equals the driving force when the resultant is zero (balanced, constant velocity — see Worked Example 3). When forces are unbalanced, friction can be smaller than the driving force, exactly as in Diagram 3 and Worked Example 2 — there is no rule that forces friction to automatically match whatever the driving force happens to be.

Hint 1
Check the directions first — are these two forces acting the same way, or opposite ways?
Hint 2
Same direction means the forces add.
Full solution
120 + 80 = 200 N, in the direction they both pushed.
2. A sled has a 300 N pull forward and 180 N friction backward. Calculate the resultant force.
Hint 1
These two forces act in opposite directions, so they subtract.
Hint 2
The answer takes the direction of the larger force.
Full solution
300 − 180 = 120 N, in the direction of the pull (the larger force).
3. A vase sits undisturbed on a shelf. Its weight is 40 N. State the normal contact force from the shelf, and whether the forces on the vase are balanced.
Hint 0 Foundation
The vase isn't moving at all, so the forces on it must be balanced — that alone tells you the normal contact force has to match the weight exactly.
Hint 1
The vase isn't accelerating in any direction, so the vertical forces on it must be balanced.
Full solution
Normal contact force = 40 N upward. The forces are balanced (resultant = 0 N), which is consistent with the vase staying still.
Higher 4. A shopping trolley has a 60 N push force forward and 25 N friction backward. It also has a weight of 80 N and a normal contact force of 80 N. Calculate the horizontal resultant force, and state whether the vertical forces are balanced.
Hint 1
Treat the horizontal and vertical forces completely separately.
Hint 2
Horizontal: 60 N forward, 25 N backward. Vertical: 80 N vs. 80 N.
Full solution
Horizontal resultant = 60 − 25 = 35 N forward. Vertical: 80 N and 80 N are equal and opposite, so the vertical forces are balanced (0 N resultant).
