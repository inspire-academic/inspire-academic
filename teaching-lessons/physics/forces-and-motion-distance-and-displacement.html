Distance and Displacement

Distance and Displacement

GCSE Physics · Forces and Motion, Lesson 1 of 8 AQA · Edexcel Estimated time: 35–40 min

By the end of this lesson you will be able to describe an object's motion using distance and displacement, and explain why these two quantities are not always the same number.

  • Define distance as a scalar quantity and displacement as a vector quantity.
  • Explain why a round trip can have a large distance but zero displacement.
  • Read and construct simple displacement diagrams on a straight line and in two dimensions.
  • Higher Combine signed 1D displacements along a single axis and use Pythagoras' theorem for a right-angle path.
Foundation pathway — what you need to master

This lesson has one focus for you: being able to work out distance and displacement for a journey, every time, without mixing them up. By the end, you should be confident with:

  • Saying which one is the scalar and which is the vector, in your own words.
  • Adding up every leg of a journey to find the distance.
  • Finding the straight-line displacement for a simple journey — including the round-trip case, where it's zero.
  • Using Pythagoras' theorem to find displacement when a journey has two legs at right angles.
  • Always writing a direction with your displacement answer.

3Core Lesson

First, a concrete example — before any definitions

Imagine you walk from your front door to a shop 200 m away, then walk straight back home along the same path.

Everyone agrees your legs did real work: you covered 200 m going, then another 200 m coming back, so 400 m of walking in total. That total — the actual ground covered — is what physics calls distance.

But if someone asks "where did you end up, compared to where you started?", the answer is: back at your own front door — no distance between start and finish at all. That's what physics calls displacement: not how much ground you covered, but how far (and which way) your finishing point is from your starting point.

Same journey, two different honest answers, because the two questions being asked are genuinely different. That's the whole idea this lesson is built on — the definitions below just make it precise.

Position and reference point

Before you can describe how something has moved, you need a fixed reference point — a starting position everything else is measured from. In a classroom, that might be the door; on a number line, it's usually zero. Every distance and every displacement in this lesson is measured relative to a stated reference point.

Distance — a scalar quantity

Distance is the total length of the path an object has travelled, measured in metres (m). It only has size (magnitude) — it doesn't care which way you were facing or which direction you ended up. Quantities like this, with magnitude only, are called scalar quantities.

Displacement — a vector quantity

Displacement is the straight-line distance from an object's starting position to its finishing position, together with the direction of that straight line. Because it needs both a size and a direction, displacement is a vector quantity. A displacement of "500 m" is an incomplete answer — "500 m east" is a complete one.

Key words, in plain terms

Magnitude just means "size" — how big a number is, with no direction attached. Scalar = magnitude only (like distance: "500 m"). Vector = magnitude and direction, both required (like displacement: "500 m east"). A quick check for any answer you write: if it needs a compass direction, +/− sign, or word like "north"/"forward" to be complete, it's a vector — write the direction down every time, not just when you remember to.

Why a round trip breaks people's intuition

If you walk 250 m to a shop and then walk the same 250 m back home, you have travelled a distance of 500 m — your legs did real work and your shoes wore down by that much. But your displacement is 0 m, because your finishing position is identical to your starting position. Distance only grows; displacement depends only on where you end up.

Picture it this way

Think of walking from your classroom to the school office and back at break time. Your legs cover real ground both ways — that's your distance. But when you're back at your desk, you're exactly where you started — your displacement is zero. Distance answers "how much ground did I cover?" Displacement answers "how far — and which way — am I from where I began?"

Signed displacement Higher

On a single straight line, it's common to assign one direction as positive and the opposite as negative — for example, "east" as positive and "west" as negative. A displacement of +8 m means 8 m in the positive direction; a displacement of −3 m means 3 m in the negative direction. Signed displacements can be added directly: a walk of +8 m followed by −3 m gives a net displacement of +5 m, even though the total distance walked was 11 m.

5Worked Examples

A method for any distance/displacement question

Before diving into a problem, break it down the same way every time: 1) Read the journey leg by leg — how many legs, and which direction is each one? 2) For distance: add up every leg's length, ignoring direction completely. 3) For displacement: find the straight-line gap between the very first position and the very last position — if two legs are at right angles, that means Pythagoras' theorem. 4) Always finish a displacement answer with its direction — a number on its own is only half the answer. Watch for this pattern in every worked example below.

Example 0 — the simplest case, one step at a time Foundation

Problem
Ade walks 50 m from his classroom to the school office, then walks the same 50 m back to class. Find his distance travelled and his displacement.
Step 1 — list the legs
Two legs: classroom → office (50 m), then office → classroom (50 m).
Step 2 — find the distance
Distance adds up every leg, regardless of direction: 50 m + 50 m = 100 m.
Step 3 — find the displacement
Displacement only compares the very start to the very finish. Ade started in the classroom and finished in the classroom — the same point.
Answer
Distance = 100 m. Displacement = 0 m.
Common wrong method: giving the displacement as 100 m (the same as the distance) or 50 m (just one leg). Whenever start and finish are the exact same point, displacement is 0 m — it doesn't matter how far you actually walked to get there.

Example 1 — Straight-line walk

Problem
Amaka walks 300 m due east along a straight road. Find her distance travelled and her displacement.
Reasoning
The path is a single straight line in one direction, so distance and displacement have the same magnitude — only displacement also needs a direction stated.
Answer
Distance = 300 m. Displacement = 300 m east.
Common wrong method: writing the displacement as "300 m" with no direction. Even when the size is correct, a displacement answer without a direction is incomplete — it's a vector, not a scalar.

Example 2 — Right-angle detour

Problem
Tunde walks 400 m north, then turns and walks 300 m east. Find the total distance travelled and the magnitude of his displacement.
Known
Leg 1 = 400 m north. Leg 2 = 300 m east. The two legs are at right angles.
Distance
400 m + 300 m = 700 m.
Displacement magnitude
The displacement is the straight line from the start to the finish — the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle with sides 400 m and 300 m:
s = √(400² + 300²) = √(160000 + 90000) = √250000 = 500 m
Direction Higher
The angle θ from north, measured toward east: tan θ = 300 / 400, so θ = tan⁻¹(0.75) ≈ 36.9°. Displacement ≈ 500 m at 037° (N36.9°E).
Common wrong method: adding 400 m + 300 m and calling that the displacement (700 m). This finds the distance, not the displacement — it ignores that the two legs point in different directions and don't lie on the same line.

Example 3 — Round trip

Problem
A cyclist rides 1.2 km from home to school, then rides the same route back home. Find the total distance and the displacement.
Reasoning
The outward and return legs cover the same 1.2 km, so the total distance is the sum of both legs. The finishing position is identical to the starting position, so the straight-line separation between start and finish is zero.
Answer
Distance = 1.2 km + 1.2 km = 2.4 km. Displacement = 0 km.
Common wrong method: giving the displacement as 2.4 km (the whole round trip) or 1.2 km (just one leg) — both confuse displacement with distance. Displacement depends only on the straight-line gap between the very first position and the very last position, which here are the same point, so it must be zero regardless of how far the cyclist actually rode.

Example 4 — Signed 1D displacement Higher

Problem
A lift starts at the ground floor. It travels up 12 m, then down 5 m. Taking "up" as positive, find the distance travelled and the net displacement.
Distance
12 m + 5 m = 17 m (distance ignores sign — both legs add).
Displacement
(+12 m) + (−5 m) = +7 m, i.e. 7 m above the ground floor.
Common wrong method: adding 12 + 5 = 17 m and calling that the net displacement. That finds the distance — it throws away the fact that the second leg is in the opposite direction. Displacement must keep the signs through the whole calculation.

Example 5 — Combining several displacements in 2D Higher

Problem
A drone flies three legs from its base: 3 km east, then 6 km north, then 5 km east. Find the magnitude of its total displacement from base.
Reasoning
This isn't a simple two-leg right-angle problem like Example 2 — there are three legs. But two of them (both east) lie along the same line, so they can be combined first: 3 km + 5 km = 8 km east. That leaves exactly two legs at right angles — 8 km east and 6 km north — which is now the same shape as Example 2.
Calculation
s = √(8² + 6²) = √(64 + 36) = √100 = 10 km
Answer
Displacement = 10 km (at an angle found the same way as Example 2, if a direction is asked for).
Common wrong methods: (1) adding all three legs (3 + 6 + 5 = 14 km) and calling that the displacement — that's the distance. (2) Applying Pythagoras directly to the three raw numbers without combining the same-direction legs first — Pythagoras only works on exactly two components at right angles, so legs sharing a line must always be combined into one before the triangle step.

6Misconception Clinic

Eight things students commonly get wrong about this topic. Open each one and check whether you've thought this yourself.

✕"Distance and displacement are always the same thing."
They're only equal in size when the journey is a single straight line in one direction. As soon as a path bends, doubles back, or changes direction, distance (the path length) becomes larger than the displacement (the straight-line separation). Check the shape of the path before assuming they match.
✕"If you end up back where you started, you haven't travelled any distance."
Your legs (or wheels, or wings) still covered real ground — that's the distance, and it's never negative and never resets to zero just because you looped back. It's only the displacement that becomes zero on a round trip, because displacement only cares about your final position relative to your start.
✕"Displacement is just a fancier word for distance."
Displacement isn't a synonym — it's a different kind of quantity. Distance is a scalar (size only); displacement is a vector (size and direction). A correct displacement answer without a direction is an incomplete answer in an exam.
✕"A negative displacement means the object didn't really move."
A negative sign just tells you the direction relative to whichever direction was chosen as positive — it says nothing about whether movement happened. −5 m means "5 m in the negative direction," which is just as real a movement as +5 m.
✕"You can add distances and displacements the same way."
Distances add by simple arithmetic — every leg makes the total bigger. Displacements add as vectors: two displacements in opposite directions can partly or fully cancel out, which is exactly why a round trip can have 500 m of distance and 0 m of displacement.
✕"Displacement can never equal distance."
This overcorrects the first misconception. When a journey is a single straight line in one consistent direction (Diagram 1), the displacement magnitude and the distance are equal — displacement is always less than or equal to distance, not always strictly less.
✕"You can give a displacement as just a number, without a direction."
A magnitude alone (e.g. "500 m") describes the size but leaves out half of what makes it a vector. A complete displacement answer states both the size and the direction — "500 m east," or, on a signed line, a value with the correct + or − sign.
✕"Speed and velocity are just other names for distance and displacement."
They're related but not the same kind of quantity. Distance and displacement describe how far something has moved; speed and velocity describe how fast it moved (distance or displacement per unit time). Speed pairs with distance (both scalars); velocity pairs with displacement (both vectors) — you'll meet this properly in Lesson 2.
✕Higher "A negative displacement means I've made a mistake — I should make it positive."
A negative sign is not an error to correct — it's information about direction. It only means the object ended up on the "negative" side of whichever direction was chosen as positive. For example, on a straight test track with "east" taken as positive, a car that finishes 6 m to the west of where it started has a displacement of exactly −6 m — not +6 m, and not a working error. Flipping the sign to "fix" it would actually make the answer wrong, since it would now claim the car ended up east of its start when it really ended up west. Only check your working if the size of the number looks wrong — a negative sign on its own is never, by itself, a red flag.
✕Higher "Changing which direction I call positive changes where the object actually ends up."
The positive/negative convention is a choice made after the journey, purely for how the answer is written down — it can't reach back and change what actually happened. For example, take a lift that goes up 12 m then down 5 m (Worked Example 4). Taking "up" as positive gives a net displacement of +7 m. If "down" had been chosen as positive instead, the exact same real journey would be written as −7 m — the sign flips, but the lift ends up in exactly the same real place either time: 7 m above where it started. Changing the convention only changes how the answer is labelled, never the physical outcome.

Extra support Warm-up. Ade walks 50 m to the school office, then walks the same 50 m back to class. What is his distance travelled, and what is his displacement?
Hint 1
Start by writing down each leg of the journey separately: 50 m one way, then 50 m back.
Hint 2
Distance: add the two legs together — distance never cares about direction.
Hint 3
Displacement: compare Ade's starting seat to where he ends up. Same place?
Full solution
Distance = 50 m + 50 m = 100 m. Displacement = 0 m, because Ade ends up exactly where he started.
1. A runner completes one lap of a 400 m circular track, finishing at the exact point she started. State her distance travelled and her displacement.
Hint 1
Distance is simply the length of track covered — you don't need to know anything about the shape of the track for this part.
Hint 2
For displacement, compare her starting position to her finishing position. Are they the same point?
Full solution
Distance = 400 m (the full lap). Displacement = 0 m, because start and finish are the same point on the track.
2. A hiker walks 6 km south, then 8 km east. Find the distance travelled and the magnitude of the displacement.
Hint 0 Foundation
Before calculating anything, write the two legs down separately with their directions: "6 km south" on one line, "8 km east" on the next. Everything else in this question is just two smaller steps applied to those two legs.
Hint 1
Distance: just add the two legs together — direction doesn't matter for distance.
Hint 2
Displacement: the two legs are at right angles, so use Pythagoras' theorem: s = √(a² + b²).
Full solution
Distance = 6 km + 8 km = 14 km. Displacement = √(6² + 8²) = √(36 + 64) = √100 = 10 km.
Higher 3. On a straight test track, taking "forward" as positive, a car travels +150 m, then −40 m, then +25 m. Find the total distance travelled and the net displacement.
Hint 1
For distance, ignore the signs — add the size of every leg.
Hint 2
For displacement, keep the signs and add them algebraically.
Full solution
Distance = 150 + 40 + 25 = 215 m. Displacement = (+150) + (−40) + (+25) = +135 m, i.e. 135 m forward of the start.
Higher 4. A cyclist rides 5 km north, then 5 km east, then 7 km north. Find the total distance travelled and the magnitude of her displacement.
Hint 1
This journey has three legs, not two — before reaching for Pythagoras, check whether any legs lie along the same line and can be combined first.
Hint 2
Two of the three legs point north. Combine those two into a single northward distance before doing anything else.
Hint 3
You should now have just two legs left, at right angles — treat it exactly like Worked Example 2 or Worked Example 5.
Full solution
Distance = 5 + 5 + 7 = 17 km (every leg adds, regardless of direction). For displacement: the two north legs combine to 5 km + 7 km = 12 km north; the remaining leg is 5 km east. Displacement = √(12² + 5²) = √(144 + 25) = √169 = 13 km.

Original, board-style questions with full mark schemes — not taken from any real past paper.

Q1 · Calculate · 2 marksCORE_ALL_TIERS
A delivery drone flies 120 m due north from its base, then 90 m due east to a customer's house. Calculate the magnitude of the drone's displacement from its base.
Uses Pythagoras' theorem: s = √(120² + 90²)1
s = √(14400 + 8100) = √22500 = 150 m1
Model answer & examiner note
Model answer: 150 m. Examiner note: full marks require the correct method shown (Pythagoras) even if a student makes a small arithmetic slip in the final line — award the method mark independently of the answer mark. A common error is adding 120 + 90 = 210 m, which finds the distance, not the displacement (0 marks for that route unless the distance figure is also correctly labelled as such, which does not answer the question asked).
Q2 · Describe · 2 marksCORE_ALL_TIERS
A student says: "The athlete ran 400 m, so her displacement was 400 m." Describe why this statement is not necessarily correct.
Recognises 400 m is the distance run (length of path), not automatically the displacement1
States displacement depends on the straight-line separation between start and finish (and direction) — e.g. if it's one lap of a track, displacement would be 0 m1
Model answer & examiner note
Model answer: "400 m is the distance travelled, i.e. the length of the path taken. Displacement depends only on the straight-line distance from the start point to the finish point, plus its direction. If the athlete ran one lap of a 400 m track and finished where she started, her displacement would actually be 0 m." Examiner note: reward any answer that correctly identifies distance vs. displacement as the issue, even without the specific track example.

A remote-control boat on a straight canal, taking "east" as positive, moves +85 m, then −30 m, then +15 m. Calculate (a) the total distance travelled and (b) the net displacement, including direction.
(a) Distance = 85 + 30 + 15 (signs ignored)1
(a) Distance = 130 m1
(b) Displacement = (+85) + (−30) + (+15) = +70 m1
(b) States direction: 70 m east1
Model answer & examiner note
Model answer: (a) 130 m. (b) 70 m east. Examiner note: part (b) loses a mark for a correct number with no stated direction — this is the single most common way students drop marks on vector questions at this level.
Higher Q4 · Calculate · 4 marksHIGHER_ASSESSED_ONLY
A search drone flies three legs from its base: 6 km east, then 8 km north, then 3 km east, before landing. Calculate the magnitude of the drone's total displacement from its base, giving your answer to 3 significant figures.
Recognises the two east legs must be combined before using Pythagoras: 6 + 3 = 9 km east1
Sets up Pythagoras with the combined east leg and the north leg: s = √(9² + 8²)1
s = √(81 + 64) = √1451
s = 12.0 km (3 s.f.)1
Model answer & examiner note
Model answer: 12.0 km. Examiner note: this question has three legs, not the usual two, and does not resolve to a clean whole number — both are deliberate. A student who applies Pythagoras directly to 6, 8, and 3 without first recognising that the two eastward legs share a line will not reach a sensible triangle. This is the item most likely to separate genuine understanding of how displacements combine from a memorised "two numbers, square root" routine — see Worked Example 5 for the fully modelled version of this reasoning.
Q5 · Evaluate · 3 marksCORE_ALL_TIERS
A student compares two delivery routes to the same house. Route A is a single straight line covering 5 km. Route B zigzags through side streets, covers 8 km, and ends at the exact same house as Route A. The student concludes: "Route B must have a bigger displacement than Route A, because it covers more distance." Evaluate this conclusion.
Recognises displacement depends only on the start and finish positions, not on the path taken to get there1
States that both routes start at the same point and finish at the same house, so both have the identical displacement, whatever their distances are1
Concludes the student's reasoning is incorrect: a bigger distance does not imply a bigger displacement, because the two quantities depend on different things1
Model answer & examiner note
Model answer: "The student's conclusion is incorrect. Displacement only depends on the straight-line gap between the start and finish positions — not on the path taken. Since both routes start and finish at the same two points, Route A and Route B have exactly the same displacement, even though Route B covers a longer distance (8 km vs. 5 km)." Examiner note: this question rewards evaluating the underlying reasoning, not just stating "the student is wrong" — full marks need the "why," specifically that distance and displacement depend on different things.
