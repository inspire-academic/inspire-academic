Relative Formula Mass & Moles

Relative Formula Mass & Moles

GCSE Chemistry · Quantitative Chemistry · Production Pilot #4 (cross-subject) AQA · Edexcel Estimated time: 35–45 min

By the end of this lesson you will be able to calculate a relative formula mass from a chemical formula, and use it to convert between mass and moles — answering the question this lesson is built around: if you can weigh a substance, how do you know how many particles you actually have?

  • Calculate the relative formula mass (Mr) of a compound by adding the relative atomic masses (Ar) of every atom in its formula.
  • Explain what a mole is, as a counting unit for very large numbers of particles.
  • Use the relationship moles = mass ÷ Mr to calculate the number of moles in a given mass of a substance.
  • Rearrange the same relationship to calculate mass from moles, or Mr from mass and moles.
  • Higher Use a calculated Mr to identify an unknown substance from a short list of candidates — genuine evaluation, not just calculation.
Foundation pathway — what you need to master

This lesson has one focus for you: being able to work out Mr from a formula, and use it to move between mass and moles, confidently and correctly every time. By the end, you should be confident with:

  • Adding up relative atomic masses correctly, including formulae with brackets like Ca(OH)2.
  • Saying, in your own words, roughly what a "mole" is.
  • Using moles = mass ÷ Mr to find moles when you're given a mass.
  • Rearranging the same relationship to find a mass when you're given moles.
  • Knowing that Mr itself has no units — it's a ratio, not a mass in grams.

3Core Lesson

First, a concrete example — before any definitions

A water molecule, H2O, is made of 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. On the periodic table, hydrogen's relative atomic mass is 1, and oxygen's is 16. Add up every atom's mass number: 1 + 1 + 16 = 18.

That's it — that's a relative formula mass. It's just adding up the mass numbers of every atom actually written in the formula. Everything else in this lesson builds on that one idea.

Before this lesson: relative atomic mass, briefly recapped

Every element has a relative atomic mass (Ar), found on the periodic table — a number comparing the mass of that element's atoms to other atoms, not a mass in grams by itself. This lesson doesn't re-teach where Ar comes from — it teaches you to use Ar values to work with whole compounds and real masses. The values used throughout this lesson: H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, Na = 23, Mg = 24, Al = 27, S = 32, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40. (Standard GCSE-rounded values; the exact periodic table supplied in a real exam is TO_BE_VERIFIED against each board's own data sheet.)

Stage 1 — What relative formula mass (Mr) means

The relative formula mass (Mr) of a compound is the sum of the relative atomic masses of every atom shown in its formula. For CO2: one carbon (12) plus two oxygens (16 + 16 = 32), giving Mr = 12 + 32 = 44. A subscript after an element symbol tells you how many atoms of that element are present — it must be counted that many times, not just added once.

Key words, in plain terms

A bracket with a subscript, like Ca(OH)2, means "multiply everything inside the bracket by that number." Ca(OH)2 has 1 calcium, but 2 oxygens and 2 hydrogens, because the bracket's own subscript multiplies every atom inside it. A quick check for any formula: identify every distinct element first, then count how many atoms of each one are actually present, before adding any mass numbers.

Stage 2 — The mole: a counting unit

Atoms and molecules are far too small and far too numerous to count one at a time. A mole is simply a counting unit for particles — the same idea as "a dozen" meaning 12 of something, except a mole means a vastly larger number of particles (6.02 × 1023, known as the Avogadro constant). The genuinely useful fact for this lesson isn't the huge number itself — it's this: the mass, in grams, of one mole of any substance is numerically equal to its Mr. One mole of water (Mr = 18) has a mass of 18 g; one mole of CO2 (Mr = 44) has a mass of 44 g.

Stage 3 — From concept to relationship

Because one mole's mass in grams always equals Mr, the number of moles in any mass of a substance follows directly: the more grams you have, the more moles — in direct, simple proportion to Mr. In words: the number of moles equals the mass you have, divided by the mass of one mole (Mr).

Stage 4 — The symbolic form

Written as a formula: n = m ÷ Mr, where n is the number of moles, m is the mass in grams, and Mr is the relative formula mass. This is exactly the same relationship as Stage 3, just written symbolically — nothing new is being claimed, only a shorter way to write it down.

Stage 5 — Rearranging the relationship

The same relationship rearranges two other ways, depending on what you're asked to find: mass = moles × Mr (when you know moles and want mass), and Mr = mass ÷ moles (when you know both mass and moles, and want to work out — or check — what the substance actually is).

Stage 6 — Using Mr to identify a substance Higher

If you're given a real mass and a real number of moles — from an experiment, say — you can calculate Mr = mass ÷ moles, then compare that number against the Mr of known candidate substances to work out which one you actually have. This is genuine evaluation, not just calculation: the arithmetic only gets you a number; deciding what that number means, by comparing it against real candidates, is the harder step.

5Worked Examples

Example 0 — the simplest case, one step at a time Foundation

Question
Calculate the relative formula mass of water, H2O.
Step 1
List every element in the formula: hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O).
Step 2
Count how many atoms of each: 2 hydrogens (from the subscript "2"), 1 oxygen.
Step 3
Look up each Ar: H = 1, O = 16.
Step 4
Add up every atom's mass number, counting repeats: 1 + 1 + 16.
Answer
Mr = 18.

Common wrong approach: adding each element's Ar only once, ignoring the subscript (1 + 16 = 17) — always check the subscript first, and count that element's Ar that many times.

Example 1 — A formula with no brackets

Question
Calculate the relative formula mass of carbon dioxide, CO2.
What matters
1 carbon atom, 2 oxygen atoms.
Reasoning
Mr = Ar(C) + 2 × Ar(O).
Calculation
12 + (2 × 16) = 12 + 32 = 44
Answer
Mr = 44.

Common wrong approach: adding 12 + 16 = 28, forgetting the subscript "2" means two separate oxygen atoms, not one.

Example 2 — A formula with a bracket

Question
Calculate the relative formula mass of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2.
What matters
The bracket's subscript "2" multiplies everything inside the bracket: both the O and the H.
Reasoning
Mr = Ar(Ca) + 2 × [Ar(O) + Ar(H)].
Calculation
40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 40 + 2 × 17 = 40 + 34 = 74
Answer
Mr = 74.

Common wrong approach: only doubling the oxygen and forgetting to double the hydrogen too — 40 + 32 + 1 = 73. Representation 3 above shows this exact error side by side with the correct method.

Example 3 — Calculating moles from a mass

Question
Calculate the number of moles in 9 g of water, H2O (Mr = 18).
What matters
m = 9 g, Mr = 18.
Reasoning
n = m ÷ Mr.
Calculation
9 ÷ 18 = 0.5
Answer
0.5 mol.

Common wrong approach: dividing Mr by mass instead of mass by Mr (18 ÷ 9 = 2) — always check the relationship reads moles = mass ÷ Mr, never the reverse.

Example 4 — Rearranging to find a mass

Question
Calculate the mass of 0.2 mol of carbon dioxide, CO2 (Mr = 44).
What matters
n = 0.2 mol, Mr = 44. This time mass, not moles, is unknown, so the relationship must be rearranged.
Reasoning
mass = moles × Mr.
Calculation
0.2 × 44 = 8.8
Answer
8.8 g.

Common wrong approach: stating the answer as "44 g" — the Mr value on its own is the mass of one whole mole, not of 0.2 mol; it must still be scaled by the actual number of moles given.

Example 5 — Identifying an unknown substance Higher

Question
A 3.2 g sample of an unknown gas contains 0.1 mol. A fictional student, revising the four gases covered this term — O2 (Mr = 32), CH4 (Mr = 16), CO2 (Mr = 44), N2 (Mr = 28) — writes: "It must be CO2, because that's the one we talked about most." Identify the gas correctly.
The student's error
Guessing from familiarity rather than calculating Mr and comparing it against the actual candidates.
Correct method
Mr = mass ÷ moles = 3.2 ÷ 0.1.
Calculation
3.2 ÷ 0.1 = 32
Answer
Mr = 32, which matches O2 — the gas is oxygen, not carbon dioxide.

6Misconception Clinic

Six specific wrong beliefs this topic invites — each one named, shown why it's wrong, and tied back to a representation or worked example above.

"Mr has units of grams."
Mr is a ratio comparing masses — it has no units at all. What has units of grams is the mass of one mole of the substance, which happens to be numerically equal to Mr — see Core Lesson Stage 2. Writing "Mr = 18 g" is a genuine, gradeable error, not just sloppy notation.
"A mole is just another word for one molecule."
A mole is an amount — a huge number of particles (6.02 × 1023) — not a single particle. "1 mole of water molecules" means that many separate H2O molecules, not one especially large one. See Core Lesson Stage 2.
"A bracket's subscript only applies to the atom right next to it, not everything inside the bracket."
A subscript directly after a closing bracket multiplies every atom inside that bracket, not just the nearest one. Ca(OH)2 has 2 oxygens and 2 hydrogens, not 2 oxygens and 1 hydrogen. See Representation 3 and Worked Example 2 for the exact error this produces if missed.
"The coefficient in front of a formula (like the 2 in 2H₂O) is part of the Mr calculation."
A number written in front of a whole formula (a coefficient, used later when balancing equations) means "this many separate formula units" — it is never added into the Mr calculation itself. Mr only ever comes from the subscripts inside one formula unit. 2H2O still has Mr = 18 for each water molecule; the "2" just means there are two of them.
"Relative formula mass and atomic number are the same thing."
Atomic number is the number of protons in one atom of an element — a completely different quantity found in a different part of the periodic table entry. Relative formula mass is built from relative atomic mass (Ar), summed across a whole formula — never from atomic number.
Higher "If a calculated Mr looks close to a familiar substance, that's good enough to identify it."
An Mr calculated from real experimental mass and mole data should match a candidate substance's Mr exactly (allowing only for genuine rounding), not just approximately. See Worked Example 5 and Guided/Exam Practice's identification questions — guessing from a "similar-sounding" number is exactly the error those questions are designed to catch.

Extra support Warm-up. Calculate the relative formula mass of sodium chloride, NaCl (Na = 23, Cl = 35.5).
Hint 1
NaCl has one atom of each element — no subscripts to worry about.
Hint 2
Mr = Ar(Na) + Ar(Cl) = 23 + 35.5.
Full solution
Mr = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5.
1. Calculate the relative formula mass of sodium carbonate, Na2CO3 (Na = 23, C = 12, O = 16).
Hint 1
There are 2 sodium atoms, 1 carbon atom, and 3 oxygen atoms.
Hint 2
Mr = (2 × 23) + 12 + (3 × 16).
Full solution
Mr = 46 + 12 + 48 = 106.
2. Calculate the number of moles in 5 g of magnesium oxide, MgO (Mg = 24, O = 16).
Hint 0 Foundation
Do this in two separate steps: first find Mr for MgO, then use n = m ÷ Mr with that Mr value — don't try to do both at once.
Hint 1
First find Mr(MgO) = 24 + 16 = 40.
Hint 2
n = m ÷ Mr = 5 ÷ 40.
Full solution
Mr(MgO) = 40. n = 5 ÷ 40 = 0.125 mol.
Higher 3. Calculate the mass of 0.05 mol of aluminium oxide, Al2O3 (Al = 27, O = 16).
Hint 1
First find Mr(Al2O3): there are 2 aluminium atoms and 3 oxygen atoms.
Hint 2
Mr = (2 × 27) + (3 × 16) = 54 + 48 = 102. Now use mass = moles × Mr.
Full solution
Mr(Al2O3) = 102. Mass = 0.05 × 102 = 5.1 g.

Q1 · State · 1 markCORE_ALL_TIERS
State what the relative formula mass of a compound represents.
The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in the formula (of the compound)1
Model answer & examiner note
Model answer: the sum of the relative atomic masses of every atom shown in the formula. Examiner note: a common answer that loses the mark is simply "the mass of the compound" — without "relative" and "sum of atomic masses," the definition is too vague for the mark.

Higher Q4 · Identify and explain · 3 marksHIGHER_ASSESSED_ONLY
A student's working for the relative formula mass of magnesium nitrate, Mg(NO3)2 (Mg = 24, N = 14, O = 16), is shown: "Mr = 24 + 14 + (3 × 16) = 24 + 14 + 48 = 86." Identify the error in this working and give the correct relative formula mass.
Identifies the error: the bracket's subscript (2) was applied to oxygen but not to nitrogen — both N and O are inside the bracket and must both be doubled1
Correct method: Mr = 24 + (2 × 14) + (2 × 3 × 16) = 24 + 28 + 961
Correct answer: Mr = 1481
Model answer & examiner note
Model answer: the error is only doubling the oxygen atoms, not the nitrogen atom, even though both are inside the bracket. Correct Mr = 148. Examiner note: this is the exact bracket error named in the Misconception Clinic and shown side by side for Ca(OH)2 in Representation 3 — recognising the same error pattern in a new formula is the genuine transfer this question tests.
Q5 · Calculate · 3 marksCORE_ALL_TIERS
Calculate the mass of 0.15 mol of sodium chloride, NaCl (Mr = 58.5). Give your answer to 3 significant figures.
mass = moles × Mr1
= 0.15 × 58.5 = 8.7751
= 8.78 g (3 s.f.)1
Model answer & examiner note
Model answer: 8.78 g. Examiner note: the raw calculation gives 8.775, which rounds to 8.78 (not 8.77) to 3 significant figures — a genuine, frequently-tested rounding step, not just a formality.
Q6 · Describe · 2 marksCORE_ALL_TIERS
Describe the difference between "relative formula mass" and "the mass, in grams, of one mole" of the same substance.
Relative formula mass has no units — it is a ratio/number1
The mass of one mole is measured in grams, and is numerically equal to Mr, but is a genuine physical mass, not a ratio1
Model answer & examiner note
Model answer: Mr is a unitless ratio; the mass of one mole is a real mass in grams that happens to share the same number as Mr. Examiner note: directly tests Misconception Clinic card 1 — "Mr has units of grams" is one of the most common losable marks in this topic.
