Reacting Masses from Balanced Equations

Reacting Masses from Balanced Equations

GCSE Chemistry · Quantitative ChemistryAQA · Edexcel40–50 minutes

A balanced equation is a quantitative promise. Its coefficients show an exact particle ratio—and therefore an exact mole ratio. This lesson answers: How can a balanced equation predict the mass of a substance that reacts or forms?

  • Interpret coefficients as mole ratios.
  • Convert known mass to moles using moles = mass ÷ Mr.
  • Apply the balanced-equation ratio.
  • Convert wanted moles to mass using mass = moles × Mr.
  • Explain why coefficients are not direct mass ratios.
  • Higher Evaluate unfamiliar multi-step methods.
Foundation pathway

Use one route every time: known mass → known moles → coefficient ratio → wanted moles → wanted mass.

3Core Lesson

Before this lesson—mass and moles

You already know n = m ÷ Mr and m = n × Mr. A reacting-mass calculation connects two of those conversions using a balanced equation.

Stage 1—coefficients are mole ratios

In 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO, the mole ratio Mg:O2:MgO is 2:1:2. The unwritten coefficient before O2 is 1.

Stage 2—convert the known mass to moles

Find Mr for the known substance, then divide mass by Mr. Equations compare particles, so grams must become moles before the ratio is used.

Stage 3—cross the equation

Use wanted moles = known moles × wanted coefficient ÷ known coefficient. Writing “wanted over known” prevents an inverted ratio.

Stage 4—convert wanted moles to mass

Find Mr for the wanted substance and multiply. Keep unrounded values in the calculator until the final answer.

Stage 5—evaluate the result Higher

Check the ratio direction, Mr values and conservation of mass. A product can outweigh one named reactant because another reactant also contributed atoms.

5Worked Examples

One stable method; progressively less familiar coefficient ratios.

Example 1—thermal decomposition, 1:1

Question
CaCO3 → CaO + CO2. Find CO2 formed from 10.0 g CaCO3. Mr: CaCO3 = 100; CO2 = 44.
Known moles
10.0 ÷ 100 = 0.100 mol CaCO3.
Ratio
1:1, so 0.100 mol CO2.
Wanted mass
0.100 × 44 = 4.40 g CO2.

Example 2—magnesium gains oxygen

Question
2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. Find MgO formed from 4.8 g Mg. Ar: Mg = 24, O = 16.
Known moles
4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.20 mol Mg.
Ratio
2:2 = 1:1, so 0.20 mol MgO.
Wanted mass
Mr(MgO) = 40; 0.20 × 40 = 8.0 g.

Check: oxygen contributes the extra 3.2 g in the product.

Example 3—a 1:2 product ratio

Question
CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O. Find water formed from 16 g CH4. Mr: CH4 = 16; H2O = 18.
Known moles
16 ÷ 16 = 1.0 mol CH4.
Ratio
1:2, so 2.0 mol H2O.
Wanted mass
2.0 × 18 = 36 g.

Example 4—working backwards

Question
N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3. Find N2 needed for 34 g NH3. Mr: NH3 = 17; N2 = 28.
Product moles
34 ÷ 17 = 2.0 mol NH3.
Reverse ratio
1:2, so 1.0 mol N2.
Reactant mass
1.0 × 28 = 28 g.

Example 5—a 4:2 ratio Higher

Question
4Al + 3O2 → 2Al2O3. Find product from 5.4 g Al. Ar: Al = 27, O = 16.
Known moles
5.4 ÷ 27 = 0.20 mol Al.
Ratio
0.20 × 2 ÷ 4 = 0.10 mol Al2O3.
Wanted mass
Mr = 102; 0.10 × 102 = 10.2 g.

6Misconception Clinic

“Coefficients give a mass ratio.”
They give particle and mole ratios. Equal moles of different substances usually have different masses.
“I can balance by changing subscripts.”
Changing a subscript changes the substance. Change coefficients in front of complete formulae only.
“I can apply the ratio directly to grams.”
Convert grams to moles first; use the ratio; then convert wanted moles back to grams.
“The ratio always multiplies.”
Direction matters. Multiply by wanted coefficient ÷ known coefficient.
“A heavier product breaks conservation.”
Another reactant may have added atoms. Compare the product with all reactants together.
“I should round after each step.”
Keep calculator values through the whole route and round the final answer unless instructed otherwise.

Guided Practice

1. 2Na + Cl2 → 2NaCl. Calculate NaCl formed from 4.6 g Na. Ar: Na = 23, Cl = 35.5.
Hint 1
4.6 ÷ 23 = 0.20 mol Na.
Hint 2
Na:NaCl is 2:2, so moles stay 0.20.
Full solution
Mr(NaCl) = 58.5. Mass = 0.20 × 58.5 = 11.7 g.
2. N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3. Calculate NH3 formed from 14 g N2. Mr: N2 = 28; NH3 = 17.
Hint 1
14 ÷ 28 = 0.50 mol N2.
Hint 2
Multiply by 2 ÷ 1 at the ratio step.
Full solution
1.0 mol NH3 forms, with mass 17 g.
Higher 3. 2Al + 3Cl2 → 2AlCl3. Calculate Al needed to make 26.7 g AlCl3. Mr(AlCl3) = 133.5; Ar(Al) = 27.
Full solution
26.7 ÷ 133.5 = 0.20 mol AlCl3. Ratio Al:AlCl3 is 2:2, so 0.20 mol Al. Mass = 0.20 × 27 = 5.4 g.
