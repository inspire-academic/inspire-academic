Electrolysis

Central question: how can an electric current make ions move, gain or lose electrons, and become new substances at two different electrodes?

AQA + Edexcel shared coreBoth tiers~50 minutesOriginal assessment

Your Foundation mastery target

By the end, you can label cathode negative and anode positive, move each ion to the correct electrode, and use a short decision rule to predict both products. You do not need to guess from the formula.

What you will master

  • Define electrolysis and electrolyte.
  • Explain why cations move to the negative cathode and anions move to the positive anode.
  • Predict products from molten binary ionic compounds.
  • Predict products from aqueous solutions with inert electrodes, including ions supplied by water.
  • Explain reduction at the cathode and oxidation at the anode.
  • Higher Construct and balance unfamiliar electrode half-equations.

Before this lesson

You should know that ionic compounds contain cations and anions, that ions have charges, and that ions are fixed in a solid but mobile when molten or dissolved.

Scope boundary

This lesson builds the shared AQA/Edexcel reasoning core. Detailed aluminium-cell engineering, copper purification and electroplating belong in later board/application lessons.

3Core Lesson

Follow the charge carriers. Electrolysis becomes predictable when you separate what happens in the wires from what happens inside the electrolyte.

Concrete first: imagine two sorting stations

The negative cathode attracts positive ions; the positive anode attracts negative ions. Each electrode then transfers electrons, turning arriving ions into products.

What electrolysis is

Electrolysis is the decomposition of an electrolyte using electrical energy from a direct current supply. An electrolyte is an ionic compound that is molten or dissolved in water, so its ions are free to move and carry charge through the liquid.

Solid ionic compounds do not conduct: they contain charged ions, but those ions are locked in a lattice and cannot move.

Two charge pathways, not one

Electrons move through the metal wires and electrodes. Inside the electrolyte, ions move: cations migrate to the cathode and anions migrate to the anode. Do not draw electrons swimming across the solution.

Electrode signs and ion direction

In an electrolytic cell, the cathode is negative and attracts cations. The anode is positive and attracts anions. The power supply maintains that polarity.

Cations → cathode (−)   |   Anions → anode (+)

Electron transfer at the surfaces

At the cathode, positive ions gain electrons: reduction. At the anode, negative ions lose electrons: oxidation. A useful memory is RED CAT; AN OX—reduction at cathode, anode oxidation.

reduction = gain of e−   |   oxidation = loss of e−

Molten compounds: one choice at each electrode

A molten binary ionic compound contains only its metal cation and non-metal anion. The metal forms at the cathode; the non-metal forms at the anode. For molten PbBr2, Pb2+ becomes lead and Br− becomes bromine, Br2.

Aqueous solutions: water adds competitors

In water, the dissolved compound's ions are joined by H+ and OH−. That creates competition.

The aqueous decision rule

Cathode: if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen, make hydrogen; otherwise make the metal.
Anode: if a halide ion (Cl−, Br−, I−) is present, make the halogen; otherwise make oxygen.

Inert means the electrodes do not join the reaction

Graphite or platinum electrodes provide conducting surfaces without supplying the product particles. This matters: reactive electrodes can change the chemistry, which is why copper-electrode purification is a separate application.

Why industry accepts the energy cost

Metals more reactive than carbon, including aluminium, cannot be extracted from their oxides by carbon reduction. Electrolysis can reduce their ions, but melting compounds and driving current uses substantial energy. Detailed aluminium process differences are outside this shared lesson.

Half-equations are charge audits Higher

A half-equation must conserve atoms and charge. Put electrons on the left for reduction and on the right for oxidation. The electron number follows the ionic charge, not a memorised pattern.

Mg2+ + 2e− → Mg   |   2Cl− → Cl2 + 2e−

5Worked Examples

Example 0 — molten sodium chloride, one decision at a time Foundation

Question
Predict the products of molten NaCl.
Known
Only Na+ and Cl− are mobile.
Reasoning
Na+ goes to cathode (−); Cl− goes to anode (+).
Working
metal cation → metal; halide anion → diatomic halogen.
Answer
Sodium at the cathode; chlorine, Cl2, at the anode.

Common wrong approach: sending Na+ to the positive electrode because it is positive. Why wrong: opposite charges attract.

Example 1 — molten lead bromide

Question
Predict both products from molten PbBr2.
Known
Pb2+ and Br−; no water is present.
Reasoning
Pb2+ is reduced at cathode. Br− is oxidised at anode.
Working
Pb2+ → Pb; two bromide ions form Br2.
Answer
Lead at cathode; bromine at anode.

Common wrong approach: writing Br as the product. Why wrong: bromine is a diatomic element, Br2.

Example 2 — aqueous copper(II) chloride, inert electrodes

Question
Predict the products of CuCl2(aq).
Known
Cu2+, Cl−, plus H+ and OH− from water.
Reasoning
Copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so Cu forms at cathode. Chloride is a halide, so chlorine forms at anode.
Working
cathode choice Cu/H → Cu; anode halide test → Cl2.
Answer
Copper at cathode; chlorine at anode.

Common wrong approach: ignoring H+/OH− because they are absent from CuCl2. Why wrong: aqueous means water contributes competing ions.

Example 3 — aqueous sodium sulfate, inert electrodes

Question
Predict the products of Na2SO4(aq).
Known
Na is more reactive than H; sulfate is not a halide.
Reasoning
Hydrogen wins at cathode. With no halide, oxygen forms at anode.
Working
cathode Na/H → H2; anode no halide → O2.
Answer
Hydrogen at cathode; oxygen at anode.

Common wrong approach: predicting sodium and sulfur from the salt formula. Why wrong: aqueous discharge rules select water-derived products here.

Example 4 — unfamiliar half-equations Higher

Question
Write half-equations for molten MgCl2.
Known
Mg2+ gains electrons; Cl− loses electrons and chlorine is Cl2.
Reasoning
Two electrons neutralise Mg2+. Two chloride ions are required per Cl2.
Working
Mg2+ + 2e− → Mg; 2Cl− → Cl2 + 2e−.
Answer
Both atoms and charge balance.

Common wrong approach: Mg2+ + e− → Mg. Why wrong: left-side charge would be +1, not 0.

6Misconception Clinic

“Electrons cross the solution.”

Electrons travel in metal wires/electrodes. Mobile ions carry charge through the electrolyte.

“Positive ions go to positive.”

Cations go to the negative cathode. Opposite charges attract.

“Anode is always negative.”

In this electrolytic cell the power supply makes the anode positive and cathode negative.

“Molten and aqueous are the same.”

Water adds H+ and OH−, changing the candidates for discharge.

“The salt formula gives aqueous products.”

Use the cathode reactivity comparison and anode halide rule; do not simply split the formula.

“OIL RIG is just a location mnemonic.”

Oxidation and reduction describe actual electron loss/gain. Check electron placement to prove each label.

“Atoms swim to electrodes.”

Charged ions migrate. Neutral atoms or molecules form only after discharge at an electrode.

“Balanced atoms means balanced equation.”

A half-equation must also balance total charge. Count charge on both sides explicitly.

Quick Reminder

Electrode signs

Cations → cathode (−). Anions → anode (+).

Electron transfer

Reduction at cathode = gain e−. Oxidation at anode = loss e−.

Aqueous rule

Cathode: metal below hydrogen → metal; otherwise H2. Anode: halide → halogen; otherwise O2.
