Percentage Yield

Percentage Yield

GCSE Chemistry · Quantitative ChemistryAQA · Edexcel40–50 minutes

Central question

Why is the mass collected in the laboratory often less than the maximum mass predicted by chemistry?

  • Distinguish theoretical yield from actual yield.
  • Calculate percentage yield using actual yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100.
  • Rearrange the relationship to find an actual or theoretical yield.
  • Explain why yield is reduced by incomplete reactions, practical losses and side reactions.
  • Diagnose an apparent yield above 100%.
  • Higher Calculate theoretical mass from a balanced equation before finding yield.
Foundation pathway

The theoretical yield is supplied. Your route is identify → substitute → calculate → interpret.

3Core Lesson

1. Two different masses

Theoretical yield is the maximum mass of product predicted from the equation, assuming complete conversion and no losses. Actual yield is the mass of product actually collected and measured.

percentage yield = actual yield / theoretical yield × 100

The numerator is actual because yield asks: “What fraction of the maximum did we actually obtain?” Both masses must use the same unit.

2. Why actual yield is usually lower

A reaction may be incomplete or reversible, some product may remain on apparatus or be lost during filtration and transfer, and competing reactions may form other products. These mechanisms reduce the desired dry product collected.

A low yield describes the amount of desired product recovered. It does not by itself tell you which loss mechanism occurred.

3. What if the answer exceeds 100%?

A genuine percentage yield cannot exceed 100% of the theoretical maximum. A result such as 108% means the measured “product” includes extra mass—perhaps water, solvent or an impurity—or that a measurement/calculation is wrong.

Do not report “excellent yield”. Diagnose wet or impure product, measurement error, or an incorrect theoretical yield.

4. Predict first, compare second Higher

When theoretical yield is not supplied, use the balanced equation: mass → moles → coefficient ratio → moles → theoretical mass. Only then compare actual with theoretical.

5Worked Examples

The structure stays stable while the unknown changes.

Example 1—calculate percentage yield

Question
Theoretical yield = 45 g; actual yield = 36 g.
Substitute
36 ÷ 45 × 100
Answer
80%
Interpret
80% of the predicted maximum was collected; 20% was not recovered as dry desired product.

Example 2—decimal masses

Question
8.5 g product is collected when 10.0 g is predicted.
Substitute
8.5 ÷ 10.0 × 100
Answer
85%

Example 3—find actual yield

Question
Theoretical yield = 25 g; percentage yield = 72%.
Rearrange
actual = percentage × theoretical ÷ 100
Answer
72 × 25 ÷ 100 = 18 g

Example 4—find theoretical yield

Question
27 g is collected at 90% yield.
Rearrange
theoretical = actual × 100 ÷ percentage
Answer
27 × 100 ÷ 90 = 30 g
Sense check
The theoretical maximum is larger than the actual yield.

Example 5—predict, then compare Higher

Equation
CaCO3 → CaO + CO2
Prediction
10.0 g CaCO3 ÷ 100 = 0.100 mol. The 1:1 ratio predicts 0.100 mol CO2 = 4.40 g.
Actual
3.52 g CO2 is collected.
Yield
3.52 ÷ 4.40 × 100 = 80%.

6Misconception Clinic

“The larger mass goes on top.”
Actual goes on top. It should normally be the smaller mass because it is the amount collected out of the maximum predicted.
“85% means 85 g.”
A percentage has no mass unit. It means 85 parts of every 100 of the theoretical amount.
“A 108% yield is exceptionally good.”
It is not chemically valid. The product may be wet or impure, or a measurement or calculation may be wrong.
“Any low yield proves the reaction was incomplete.”
That is one possibility. Practical loss, reversibility and competing reactions can also reduce the desired product collected.
“Percentage yield and atom economy are the same.”
Yield compares actual with theoretical product. Atom economy considers how reactant atoms are distributed among products; it is outside this lesson.
“I should round the theoretical mass early.”
Keep full calculator values until the final percentage to avoid accumulating rounding error.

Guided Practice

1. A reaction has a theoretical yield of 40 g and produces 30 g. Calculate the percentage yield.
Hint 1
Put actual yield on top: 30 ÷ 40.
Full solution
30 ÷ 40 × 100 = 75%.
2. Theoretical yield is 60 g and percentage yield is 65%. Calculate the actual yield.
Hint
actual = percentage × theoretical ÷ 100.
Full solution
65 × 60 ÷ 100 = 39 g.
Higher 3. 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. 4.8 g Mg predicts 8.0 g MgO. A student collects 6.8 g. Calculate the percentage yield.
Full solution
6.8 ÷ 8.0 × 100 = 85%.
