Central question

How can we prepare pure, dry crystals of a soluble salt?

  • Choose a suitable acid and insoluble base or carbonate for a named salt.
  • Use the warm, add-to-excess, filter, concentrate and crystallise method safely.
  • Explain why excess solid is added and why the mixture is filtered.
  • Distinguish crystallisation from heating a solution to dryness.
  • Evaluate choices that affect purity and product recovery.

3Core Lesson

Prepare copper(II) sulfate crystals

Wear eye protection. Dilute sulfuric acid and copper sulfate solution can irritate eyes, and the apparatus becomes hot. Use a hot-water bath or electric heater; heat gently and do not boil.

  1. Measure dilute sulfuric acid into a beaker and warm it gently.
  2. Add black copper(II) oxide a spatula at a time, stirring after each addition.
  3. Continue until some black solid remains. This shows that all the acid has reacted and the copper(II) oxide is in excess.
  4. Filter off the excess copper(II) oxide. The blue filtrate is copper(II) sulfate solution.
  5. Transfer the filtrate to an evaporating basin and warm it gently so some water evaporates. Do not heat the solution to dryness.
  6. Stop heating and leave the concentrated solution to cool and crystallise.
  7. Filter the crystals, rinse them with a small amount of cold distilled water, then blot them dry between filter papers.

Choose the reactants

The acid supplies the negative ion in the salt:

Hydrochloric acid
makes a chloride.
Sulfuric acid
makes a sulfate.
Nitric acid
makes a nitrate.

The metal, metal oxide, metal hydroxide or metal carbonate supplies the positive metal ion. In this practical, copper(II) oxide supplies Cu²⁺.

4Representations

What is reacting?

H₂SO₄ + CuO → CuSO₄ + H₂O

This is neutralisation: acid + metal oxide → salt + water. Copper(II) sulfate is soluble, so it remains dissolved in the filtrate until water is removed and crystals form.

Carbonate route: an insoluble carbonate may be used instead of the oxide. Add it slowly because carbon dioxide is released as well as water.

5Worked Examples

Example 1 — explain every separation decision

Add excess solid
When some solid remains, every acid particle has had the opportunity to react. No unreacted acid should contaminate the crystals.
Filter
Copper(II) oxide is insoluble, so the unreacted excess stays as residue. The filtrate contains the soluble copper(II) sulfate solution.
Concentrate gently
Remove only some water. Vigorous heating can spit solution and lose product; heating completely dry may damage the salt.
Cool
The concentrated solution forms crystals as it cools.
Rinse cold
A little cold distilled water removes solution from the crystal surfaces. Much water, especially warm water, would dissolve more crystals and reduce the yield.

Example 2 — choose the correct preparation route

Make magnesium nitrate from magnesium oxide: choose nitric acid because a nitrate is required. Warm the acid, add magnesium oxide to excess, filter, concentrate, cool, filter the crystals and dry them.

Make sodium chloride from sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid: both reactants are soluble, so excess sodium hydroxide cannot be removed by filtration. Use titration to find the exact neutralising volumes, then repeat without indicator before crystallising the salt.

6Misconception Clinic

“Stop adding solid as soon as the black colour appears.”

Add and stir until a little solid remains after stirring. That persistent excess is the evidence that all acid has reacted.

“The solid left in the filter paper is the product.”

No. It is excess copper(II) oxide. The soluble salt is in the blue filtrate.

“Evaporate all the water for the best yield.”

Do not heat the solution to dryness. Concentrate it, then cool so crystals form safely and cleanly.

“This method works for any acid and alkali.”

No. If both reactants are soluble, an excess cannot be filtered off; titration is the suitable route.
