Block 1 — The Physicist's Toolkit

Every Energy calculation needs the same four tools: units and prefixes, standard form, rearranging equations, and reading graphs. This block makes those tools reliable, so that when you make a mistake later it is a physics mistake, not a maths one.

Learning goals

By the end of this block I can…

  • Units and prefixes: convert between prefixed units and SI base units (T, G, M, k, c, m, µ, n; grams to kilograms; minutes and hours to seconds), and say which way the number should change before I convert.
  • Standard form: write very large and very small numbers in standard form, read them back, and use them in a calculation on my calculator.
  • Rearranging: substitute into an equation and rearrange it to make any quantity the subject, including equations with a fraction, a square, a half or a square root.
  • Graphs: read a value from a graph by working out what one small square is worth, and find the gradient of a straight line and say what it represents.

The calculation routine

Use these seven steps for every calculation in this programme. Each worked example below is set out in these steps. Come back to this panel whenever you are stuck.

  1. GIVENList every quantity with its symbol and unit. Convert to SI units now (kg, m, s, J, W).
  2. FINDWrite the symbol of the quantity you need.
  3. RELATIONSHIPWrite the equation in symbols (from memory, or from the equation sheet if it is a given equation).
  4. REARRANGEMake the unknown the subject before putting numbers in.
  5. SUBSTITUTEPut the numbers in. This line usually earns the first mark.
  6. SOLVECalculate. Give the unit, and the significant figures asked for.
  7. CHECKIs the size sensible? Is the unit right for the quantity?

Tool 1: units and prefixes

A solar panel array on a clinic roof in Kumasi transfers about 4.5 MJ of energy in an afternoon. A large power station has an output power of 1.2 GW. The sensor circuit in a smoke alarm works at about 15 µW. All of these use the same units, the joule (J) and the watt (W). The prefix tells you the size.

SI prefixes you must know

millim10−30.001a 35 mm screw
microµ10−60.000 001a smoke alarm sensor: 15 µW
nanon10−90.000 000 001the wavelength of green light: about 500 nm

Which way? Decide before you calculate.

  • Converting to a smaller unit: you need more of them, so the number gets bigger (multiply). 3.6 MJ = 3.6 × 106 J = 3 600 000 J.
  • Converting to a bigger unit: you need fewer of them, so the number gets smaller (divide). 5000 W = 5000 ÷ 1000 = 5 kW.

Two traps in physics calculations.

  • The SI unit of mass is the kilogram, not the gram. Grams to kilograms: divide by 1000. 500 g = 0.5 kg.
  • The SI unit of time is the second. Minutes to seconds: multiply by 60. Hours to seconds: multiply by 3600 (60 × 60). 2.5 h = 2.5 × 3600 s = 9000 s.

Watch the letters: “m” is the metre (unit) and also milli (prefix), so 5 mm is 5 millimetres. The letter s is the unit “seconds” and also the symbol for distance in W = Fs. Temperature is measured in °C; a change of 1 °C is the same size as a change of 1 K.

In the GIVEN step of the routine, every quantity is converted to SI units before it goes into an equation.

Think 1 A pupil converts 3.0 km into metres and gets 0.003 m. Without calculating, how can you tell this is wrong? What is the right answer?

Tool 2: standard form

Physics numbers are often very large (the energy from a power station) or very small (the power of a sensor). Standard form writes them without long strings of zeros.

A × 10n, where 1 ≤ A < 10 and n is an integer (a whole number: positive, negative or zero).

  • Large number: 3 600 000 J. Move the decimal point until the number is between 1 and 10: 3.6. It moved 6 places, and the number is large, so n = 6: 3.6 × 106 J.
  • Small number: 0.000 35 m. The decimal point moves 4 places to give 3.5, and the number is less than 1, so n = −4: 3.5 × 10−4 m.
  • A number less than 1 always has a negative power of ten. A number of 10 or more has a positive power.

On your calculator, use the ×10x key (on some calculators, EXP). To enter 1.2 × 109, press 1.2 then ×10x then 9. Do not also press “× 10”: that makes the number 10 times too big. For a negative power, use the (−) key: 1.5 ×10x (−) 5.

Prefixes and standard form match: k = × 103, M = × 106, G = × 109, T = × 1012, m = × 10−3, µ = × 10−6, n = × 10−9.

Think 2 Why is 58 × 105 J not written in standard form? Write it correctly.

Tool 3: rearranging equations

An equation is a balance. Whatever you do to one side, you do to the other. To make a quantity the subject, undo the operations on it in reverse order.

Product form

W = F s

F is multiplied by s. To undo, divide both sides by s:

F = W ÷ s    (and s = W ÷ F)

Quotient form

P = E ÷ t

E is divided by t. To undo, multiply both sides by t:

E = P × t

Then divide both sides by P to find t: t = E ÷ P

With a half and a square

Ek = ½ m v²

To build Ek from v: square v, multiply by m, multiply by ½.

Undo in reverse order: multiply by 2, divide by m, then take the square root.

2Ek = m v² → v² = 2Ek ÷ m → v = √(2Ek ÷ m)

For m, stop after two steps: m = 2Ek ÷ v²

Rearrange before you put numbers in (the REARRANGE step). A quick unit check helps: a watt is a joule per second, so W × s = J. That tells you E = P × t, not P ÷ t. Square-root rearrangements are Higher-tier demand.

Think 3 Rearrange W = Fs to make s the subject. Say what you did to both sides.

Tool 4: reading graphs

An electric heater warms some water in an insulated beaker. Energy is transferred electrically to the heater and then by heating to the thermal energy store of the water, so its temperature rises. Graph 1 shows the temperature of the water against time.

Step 1 — before you read anything: what is one small square worth?

value of one small square = (difference between two labelled lines) ÷ (number of small squares between them)

On Graph 1, the time axis goes from 0 s to 50 s in 5 small squares, so one small square is 50 ÷ 5 = 10 s. The temperature axis goes from 0 °C to 10 °C in 5 small squares, so one small square is 2 °C.

Read a value: go straight up from the x-value to the line, then straight across to the y-axis. Count the small squares.

Gradient of a straight line:

gradient = change in y ÷ change in x = Δy ÷ Δx

  • Use two points far apart on the line, where the line crosses gridlines. Draw a big triangle.
  • The unit of the gradient is the y-unit ÷ the x-unit (°C/s, m/s, J/s).
  • Say what it represents: on a distance–time graph the gradient is the speed; on a temperature–time graph it is the rate of temperature rise; on an energy–time graph it is the power (the rate of energy transfer).
  • A value tells you how much at one moment. A gradient tells you how fast it is changing. Do not mix them up.

Worked examples

Time guide: 10 minutes (minutes 20–30 of the session)

For each tool: one example fully worked, then one faded example for you to finish. Every example uses the calculation routine.

Example 1 (fully worked) — Units: a solar panel array in Kumasi

A solar panel array on a clinic roof in Kumasi transfers 4.5 MJ of energy electrically in 2.5 hours. Calculate its average power.

Hint — recall equation (learn it): power = energy transferred ÷ time: P = E ÷ t

  1. GIVEN
    E = 4.5 MJ = 4.5 × 106 J = 4 500 000 J (MJ to J: smaller unit, bigger number). t = 2.5 h = 2.5 × 3600 s = 9000 s.
  2. FIND
    P
  3. RELATIONSHIP
    P = E ÷ t
  4. REARRANGE
    P is already the subject.
  5. SUBSTITUTE
    P = 4 500 000 J ÷ 9000 s
  6. SOLVE
    P = 500 W
  7. CHECK
    A few hundred watts is sensible for a small array of panels. Energy ÷ time gives J/s = W, the unit of power. ✓

Example 2 (faded: you do one step) — Units: heating water in a mug

250 g of water in a mug is heated in a microwave from 20 °C to 80 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/kg °C. Calculate the energy transferred to the thermal energy store of the water.

From the equation sheet: change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change ΔE = m c Δθ

(Energy equation used here only to practise the skill: you meet it properly in a later block.)

  1. GIVEN
    m = 250 g = ? kg (your step)   c = 4200 J/kg °C   Δθ = 80 °C − 20 °C = 60 °C
  2. FIND
    ΔE
  3. RELATIONSHIP
    ΔE = m c Δθ
  4. REARRANGE
    ΔE is already the subject.
  5. SUBSTITUTE
    ΔE = (your mass in kg) × 4200 J/kg °C × 60 °C
  6. SOLVE
    Use your mass to calculate ΔE: the model answer shows the result.
  7. CHECK
    If you forget to convert grams to kilograms, your answer comes out 1000 times too big: tens of kilojoules is sensible for a mug of water.
Check the model answer (only after you have written yours)
GIVEN: m = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 kg. A kilogram is a bigger unit than a gram, so the number gets smaller. Check: 0.250 × 4200 × 60 = 63 000 J (63 kJ).

Example 3 (fully worked) — Standard form: a power station

A power station has an output power of 1.2 GW. Calculate the energy it transfers in 1 hour. Give your answer in standard form.

Hint — recall equation (learn it): power = energy transferred ÷ time: P = E ÷ t

  1. GIVEN
    P = 1.2 GW = 1.2 × 109 W. t = 1 h = 3600 s.
  2. FIND
    E
  3. RELATIONSHIP
    P = E ÷ t
  4. REARRANGE
    Multiply both sides by t: E = P × t
  5. SUBSTITUTE
    E = 1.2 × 109 W × 3600 s
  6. SOLVE
    Calculator: 1.2 ×10x 9 × 3600 = 4 320 000 000 000. E = 4.32 × 1012 J
  7. CHECK
    1012 is the prefix T, so this is 4.32 TJ: a huge amount of energy, as expected for a power station. The first number, 4.32, is between 1 and 10. ✓

Example 4 (faded: you do one step) — Standard form: a smoke alarm sensor

The sensor circuit in a smoke alarm has a power of 15 µW. Calculate the energy it transfers in 1 minute. Give your answer in standard form.

Hint — recall equation (learn it): power = energy transferred ÷ time: P = E ÷ t

  1. GIVEN
    P = 15 µW = 15 × 10−6 W = 1.5 × 10−5 W. t = 1 min = 60 s.
  2. FIND
    E
  3. RELATIONSHIP
    P = E ÷ t
  4. REARRANGE
    E = P × t
  5. SUBSTITUTE
    E = 1.5 × 10−5 W × 60 s
  6. SOLVE
  7. CHECK
    A tiny power for a short time gives a tiny energy, less than 1 J, so the power of ten must be negative.
Check the model answer (only after you have written yours)
SOLVE: calculator 1.5 ×10x (−) 5 × 60 = 0.0009. E = 9.0 × 10−4 J (0.000 90 J).

Example 5 (fully worked) — Rearranging with a half and a square: a delivery van

A loaded delivery van is moving at 12 m/s. There is 216 kJ of energy in its kinetic energy store. Calculate the mass of the loaded van.

Hint — recall equation (learn it): kinetic energy = 0.5 × mass × (speed)²: Ek = ½ m v²

  1. GIVEN
    Ek = 216 kJ = 216 000 J. v = 12 m/s.
  2. FIND
    m
  3. RELATIONSHIP
    Ek = ½ m v²
  4. REARRANGE
    m was multiplied by v² and by ½. Undo in reverse order. Multiply both sides by 2: 2Ek = m v². Divide both sides by v²: m = 2Ek ÷ v²
  5. SUBSTITUTE
    m = 2 × 216 000 J ÷ (12 m/s)²
  6. SOLVE
    m = 432 000 ÷ 144 = 3000 kg
  7. CHECK
    About 3 tonnes is sensible for a loaded van. If you divided by 2 instead of multiplying, you would get 750 kg: too light for a loaded van. ✓

Example 6 (faded: you do one step) — Rearranging a quotient: a classroom heater

An electric heater in a classroom in Leeds has a power of 1.5 kW. It transfers 2.7 MJ of energy. Calculate how long it was switched on. Give your answer in minutes.

Hint — recall equation (learn it): power = energy transferred ÷ time: P = E ÷ t

  1. GIVEN
    P = 1.5 kW = 1500 W. E = 2.7 MJ = 2 700 000 J.
  2. FIND
    t
  3. RELATIONSHIP
    P = E ÷ t
  4. REARRANGE
  5. SUBSTITUTE
    Put E = 2 700 000 J and P = 1500 W into your rearranged equation.
  6. SOLVE
    t = 1800 s = 1800 ÷ 60 = 30 minutes
  7. CHECK
    Half an hour is a sensible time for a heater. If your rearranged equation gives 0.000 56, it is the wrong way up.
Check the model answer (only after you have written yours)
REARRANGE: multiply both sides by t (P t = E), then divide both sides by P: t = E ÷ P. So t = 2 700 000 J ÷ 1500 W = 1800 s.

Example 7 (fully worked) — Graphs: reading a value and finding a gradient

Use Graph 1 (above, in Tool 4). (a) What is the temperature of the water at 60 s? (b) Calculate the gradient of the line and say what it represents.

  1. GIVEN
    Scale first: one small square is 10 s across and 2 °C up. (a) t = 60 s. (b) Two points far apart on the line, on gridline crossings: (100 s, 30 °C) and (300 s, 50 °C).
  2. FIND
    (a) The temperature θ at 60 s. (b) The gradient.
  3. RELATIONSHIP
    (a) Go up from 60 s to the line, then across. (b) gradient = Δy ÷ Δx = Δθ ÷ Δt
  4. REARRANGE
    Not needed.
  5. SUBSTITUTE
    (a) From 60 s, the line is 13 small squares up: 13 × 2 °C = 26 °C. (b) gradient = (50 °C − 30 °C) ÷ (300 s − 100 s) = 20 °C ÷ 200 s
  6. SOLVE
    (a) θ = 26 °C. (b) gradient = 0.10 °C/s
  7. CHECK
    The unit is °C per second (y-unit ÷ x-unit). It represents the rate of temperature rise: the water gets 0.10 °C hotter every second. Check with the start: from 20 °C, after 60 s it should be 20 + 60 × 0.10 = 26 °C, which matches (a). ✓

Example 8 (faded: you do one step) — Graphs: reading from the time axis

Use Graph 1. (a) At what time does the water reach 44 °C? (b) By how much does the temperature rise in the first 3 minutes?

  1. GIVEN
    One small square: 10 s across, 2 °C up. (b) t = 3 min = 3 × 60 s = 180 s.
  2. FIND
    (a) the time t when θ = 44 °C. (b) the rise Δθ.
  3. RELATIONSHIP
    (a) Go across from 44 °C to the line, then down to the time axis. (b) rise = temperature at the end − temperature at the start.
  4. REARRANGE
    Not needed.
  5. SUBSTITUTE
    (a) Your step: read the time.(b) At 180 s the line is at 38 °C; at 0 s it is at 20 °C.
  6. SOLVE
    (b) rise = 38 °C − 20 °C = 18 °C
  7. CHECK
    (b) 180 s × 0.10 °C/s (the gradient from Example 7) = 18 °C ✓. Use the same gradient to check your answer to (a).
Check the model answer (only after you have written yours)
(a) 44 °C is 22 small squares up. Across to the line and down: 24 small squares along, 24 × 10 s = 240 s. Check: (44 − 20) °C ÷ 0.10 °C/s = 240 s ✓.
